Case 001 · Marine Asset Recovery

RESTORING A 1972 PORT TUG TO OPERATIONAL SERVICE

The recovery of “100 Years of Krasnovodsk” — an initiative led by the Chief Engineer, combining practical machinery restoration, team mobilisation and resource coordination.
Chief Engineer  ·  Recovery Initiative  ·  Engine-Room Teamwork  ·  Machinery Restoration

01 — The Asset

A port tug built in 1972, known as “100 Years of Krasnovodsk”, had become immobilised and was being considered for decommissioning. The stated position was that restoration was technically impossible and economically unjustifiable.

The tug performed two important functions for the port: assisting with vessel manoeuvring and supplying water and fuel to visiting vessels. Its continued operation therefore had direct operational and commercial value.

Rather than accept decommissioning as the only option, I initiated an effort to restore the vessel and return it to operational service.

02 — Initial Technical Condition

The tug had several critical systems out of service. Both main engines were immobilised, only one of the two auxiliary diesel generators was operational, and the air compressor was not serviceable. The fuel oil separator was contaminated and incomplete, with several disc-stack plates missing.

Main Propulsion

Both main engines required substantial work, including piston and piston-ring replacement, fuel-injection repairs and work on cylinder-head components and cooling systems.

Auxiliary Power

One auxiliary diesel-generator set was not operational and required restoration.

Starting Air and Compressor

The air compressor required replacement. The starting-air receivers and their valves also needed attention.

Fuel Treatment

The fuel oil separator was contaminated and missing several disc-stack plates. A leaking valve was also identified on its suction line.

Auxiliary Machinery

The ballast pump and fire pump required repair before the vessel could regain its operational capability.

Operational Context

The vessel's future was uncertain. Its recovery required coordinated work across several machinery systems and practical solutions for obtaining scarce components.

03 — Main Engine Restoration

Both main engines were restored through coordinated work by the engine-room team under my direction as Chief Engineer. The work included:

  • Replacing pistons and piston rings on both engines.
  • Removing cylinder heads, lapping valve seats and replacing defective valves where required.
  • Replacing fuel injectors and sections of high-pressure fuel piping.
  • Manufacturing replacement textolite gaskets from available sheet material.
  • Addressing the damaged seawater cooling pump on the starboard engine.
  • Cleaning the port engine heat exchanger.
  • Replacing engine filters and renewing lubricating oil.
Pistons and piston rings required for the repairs were located in the spare-parts stock of a decommissioned vessel operated by another organisation.

The reduction gears and propeller shafts were found to be in working condition and remained serviceable.

04 — Auxiliary Diesel Generator Restoration

One of the two auxiliary diesel-generator sets, model 6Ch12/14, required restoration. The work included:

  • Replacing cylinder-head gaskets.
  • Reconstructing a serviceable high-pressure fuel injection pump using components from two unserviceable pumps.
  • Replacing injector nozzles.
  • Lapping injector needle valves, pressure-testing the injectors and installing them.
  • Replacing fuel and lubricating-oil filters.
  • Cleaning the oil centrifuge.
  • Adjusting fuel injection timing.

The restored diesel generator was started successfully and subsequently tested as part of the vessel's machinery recovery.

05 — Starting Air System and Compressor

The vessel's air compressor was replaced and started. Its operation was satisfactory.

The vessel was equipped with four starting-air receivers, each rated at 30 atmospheres, supplying compressed air for starting both the main and auxiliary engines.

The valve sealing elements on all four receivers were replaced. Following the work, each receiver was checked for air leakage using a soap-solution test.

No air leakage was detected. All four starting-air receivers passed the check.

06 — Ballast and Fire Pump Restoration

Ballast Pump

The ballast pump was restored following replacement of its impeller.

Fire Pump

The fire pump was repaired. The work included replacement of the shaft seal, spring, impeller and valve on the discharge line.

Both pumps were tested after the repairs and demonstrated normal operation.

07 — Fuel Oil Separator Recovery

The fuel oil separator was found contaminated and missing several disc-stack plates. I located the missing plates aboard a foreign vessel that had arrived in port.

I negotiated an exchange with that vessel's Chief Engineer: the missing separator plates in return for an unused spare fuel-injector nozzle.

The leaking valve on the separator suction line was also replaced. The separator was fully reassembled and tested successfully.

08 — Recovery Initiative, Team Mobilisation and Resource Coordination

The initiative to restore the tug was mine. I did not accept that decommissioning was the only option. I believed that the vessel's operational capability could be recovered through coordinated technical work, practical resourcefulness and the commitment of the engine-room team.

As Chief Engineer, I personally took responsibility for mobilising the team around this objective. Bringing the crew together, establishing a common purpose and motivating them to undertake the restoration were central parts of my initiative.

I personally led the search for spare parts and, in most cases, dealt directly with other organisations to obtain serviceable components from worn-out, withdrawn or decommissioned vessels. This involved identifying what could still be used and finding practical ways to obtain the components required.

The restoration itself was a collective effort. The engine-room team carried out the technical work. I directed and coordinated the recovery activities and also took an active part in the repairs.

My contribution combined the original recovery initiative, personal mobilisation of the team, technical direction, resource acquisition and direct participation in the work.

09 — Testing and Operational Verification

The restored and repaired machinery was tested. All mechanisms underwent testing and demonstrated normal operation.

The fuel oil separator was fully assembled and tested successfully. The starting-air receivers were separately checked for leakage using a soap-solution test, with no leakage detected.

The restored auxiliary generator was started successfully. The compressor, ballast pump and fire pump also demonstrated satisfactory or normal operation following their respective repairs.

10 — Sea Trials, Docking and Register Inspection

Machinery readiness
After completion and testing of the machinery work, I reported to the Captain that the tug was ready for sea trials.
Management notification
The Captain went to the Port Fleet office and informed the Head of the Port Fleet that the vessel was ready for sea trials. The Head was astonished because he had previously been told that restoration was technically impossible and economically unjustifiable.
Sea trials
The tug performed very well during sea trials. The vessel demonstrated that its restored machinery could support its operational functions.
Dry-docking
The next stage was dry-docking, hull inspection and painting. The Captain was responsible for the docking operation. During this period, the engines and engine room were cleaned and painted. I paid particular attention to the propellers; their condition was satisfactory.
Register inspection
The vessel was presented to the Register and passed the required inspection. It received permission to continue operating. Its existing technical and operational documentation remained unchanged.
Return to service
The tug returned to its port duties. I continued working aboard the vessel after its restoration.

11 — Formal Retention and Operational Outcome

Following the vessel's presentation to the Register, port management placed the tug on the Port Fleet's balance sheet. The vessel was formally retained as a Port Fleet asset rather than being decommissioned.

The restored tug resumed its two principal port functions: assisting with vessel manoeuvring and supplying water and fuel to visiting vessels. Its return to service restored an important source of operational capacity for the port.

The crew retained their jobs, and full salaries were restored. The outcome therefore extended beyond the machinery itself: the vessel returned to productive use and the people working aboard her retained their employment.

Subsequent service — reported information

According to information I received after leaving the organisation, the tug remained in operation for approximately 12 years or possibly longer after the restoration. I cannot independently verify its complete subsequent service history or the exact duration.

13 — Professional Perspective

This case demonstrates that restoring an ageing marine vessel to operational service requires more than simply repairing or replacing machinery.

It requires initiative, a clear operational objective, technical judgement, resourcefulness and the ability to bring people together around a difficult task.

The restoration of this tug was achieved through the combined effort of the engine-room team. My role was to initiate the effort, mobilise the team, direct the recovery process, secure the necessary resources and take an active part in the practical work.

The final result was not simply a collection of repaired machines. It was the successful return of a working port vessel to operational service.
